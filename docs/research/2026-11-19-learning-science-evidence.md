# Learning science, neuroscience and assessment: checked evidence

Search date: 19 November 2026. Scope: adaptive university learning, including Master's-level work.
This is a targeted evidence review, not a preregistered systematic review or a platform efficacy study.
Recent 2024–2026 syntheses lead; older methodological sources remain where they govern valid inference.
Companion documents: [platform design](2026-11-19-adaptive-learning-platform-design.md) and
[AI tutoring evidence](2026-11-19-ai-tutoring-evidence.md).

## How claims were checked

- Search publisher sites, PubMed/PMC, author/institution repositories, and official assessment standards.
- Inspect the relevant abstract, methods/discussion passage or standard; record access limits below.
- Prefer synthesis for general recommendations and real-learner controlled studies for causal AI claims.
- Separate learning from immediate performance, prediction accuracy, motivation and interface preference.
- Effect sizes below use different comparators and populations. They cannot be added or ranked as feature ROI.
- **Supported** means the inspected passage supports the narrow statement, not that ScreenTutor is validated.
- **Partially supported** means access or generalization limits constrain the claim. Proposals remain proposals.
- Publication metadata was checked on the linked records. This is not a comprehensive retraction audit.

## P1 — Retrieval, spacing and interleaving in authentic classrooms

Ruitenburg et al. (29 October 2026), *Educational Psychology Review* 38:137, peer-reviewed meta-analysis.
[Publisher/full text](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-026-10225-5).

- **Inspected:** abstract; discussion “Retrieval Practice,” “Interleaved Practice,” and limitations.
- **Design:** 53 primary/secondary classroom studies, 233 effects, 7,303 participants; studies published 2006–2024.
- **Finding:** average retrieval g=.37, spacing g=.20, interleaving g=.40. Outcomes and comparison conditions matter.
- **Limit:** heterogeneous studies; small moderator cells and correlated moderators limit causal interpretations.
  Complex comprehension/transfer benefits were less dependable than simple retention benefits.
- **Verdict: supported.** Include retrieval and revisits, while also teaching coherent models and application.
  These are school-classroom estimates, not expected effects in Master's courses or for a particular UI.

## P2 — Retrieval compared with strong elaboration

Gonçalves, Muniz & Jaeger (30 October 2025), *Educational Psychology Review* 37:100, peer-reviewed review.
[DOI/publisher](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-025-10076-6);
[ERIC abstract](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1492680).

- **Inspected:** indexed abstract and Crossref identity/date. Full publisher access failed; no full-methods audit.
- **Finding at abstract level:** retrieval's advantage over elaborative encoding was small, g=.14.
- **Limit:** this compares retrieval with active elaboration, not ordinary rereading. It does not show retrieval is useless.
- **Verdict: partially supported, abstract-only.** Do not turn every learning objective into recall-only flashcards.

## P3 — Self-explanation in digital environments

Tan et al. (8 March 2025), *Educational Psychology Review* 37:20, peer-reviewed meta-analysis.
[Publisher](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-025-10001-x).

- **Inspected:** publisher abstract; full article is subscription content.
- **Design:** 56 studies, 204 effects; self-explanation versus no self-explanation.
- **Finding:** overall g=.46; transfer g=.33; delayed outcomes g=.35. Learner pacing moderated results.
- **Limit:** pooled settings and outcomes; moderator associations do not prove a particular prompting recipe works.
- **Verdict: partially supported, abstract-only.** Ask for explanations of consequential steps and assumptions;
  assess explanation quality rather than word count or polished language.

## P4 — Digital instructional feedback

Brummer, de Boer, Mouw & Strijbos (17 June 2024), *Learning Environments Research* 27:453–476.
[Publisher/full text](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10984-024-09501-4).

- **Inspected:** abstract; “Combining context, content, and task factors”; limitations.
- **Design:** meta-analysis of 116 interventions; overall effect .41, SE=.05.
- **Finding:** feedback effects depend on task, context and content. Process-focused feedback was promising.
- **Limit:** sparse combinations, unclear specifications in some studies, and mixed domains; delivering feedback
  does not establish that learners process or use it. No universal timing rule follows.
- **Verdict: supported.** Feedback should lead to a learner revision or next step. Avoid automatic answer delivery
  after every keystroke and avoid blanket “always immediate” or “always delayed” policies.

## P5 — Multimedia design has boundary conditions

Cromley & Chen (November 2025), *Educational Research Review* 49:100730, peer-reviewed meta-analysis.
[DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2025.100730);
[author institution abstract](https://experts.illinois.edu/en/publications/a-meta-analysis-of-richard-mayers-multimedia-learning-research-se/).

- **Inspected:** institution-hosted abstract and publication metadata; full methods not audited.
- **Design:** Mayer-authored research corpus, 92 articles/181 studies/591 effects, published 1990–2022.
- **Finding:** text-plus-diagram results were relatively consistent; effects varied by medium, principle and outcome.
- **Limit:** one research corpus; not a complete synthesis of every multimedia intervention or modern AI interface.
- **Verdict: partially supported.** Select a representation for its explanatory job, minimize irrelevant detail,
  and test whether an interactive visual adds learning value over a simpler diagram. “Rich” is not inherently better.

## P6 — Autonomy with structure

Patzak & Zhang (21 February 2025), *Educational Psychology Review* 37:17, peer-reviewed synthesis.
[Publisher/full text](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-025-09994-2).

- **Inspected:** abstract; discussion “Teacher Autonomy Support and Provision of Structure is Linked to Student Learning.”
- **Design:** 94 studies/110 effects; diverse operationalizations and school levels.
- **Finding:** autonomy support and structure can complement one another; associations with motivation and engagement
  were stronger than the small relationships with academic achievement.
- **Limit:** do not reinterpret pooled associations as causal evidence that an app choice menu raises grades.
- **Verdict: supported.** Recommend a clear next task and explain its purpose; permit a meaningful override.
  Independence means gradually developing regulation, not leaving a novice without guidance.

## P7 — Productive struggle needs instructional design

Sinha & Kapur (2021), *Review of Educational Research* 91:761–798, peer-reviewed meta-analysis.
[Publisher abstract](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.3102/00346543211019105).

- **Inspected:** publisher abstract. The full methods were not audited in this review.
- **Design:** 53 studies/166 comparisons of problem-solving-before-instruction versus the reverse sequence.
- **Finding:** conceptual/transfer benefit favored problem solving first on average, g=.36, 95% CI [.20,.51].
- **Limit:** productive failure is a designed sequence with subsequent instruction; grade level and task matter.
  Some younger-learner/domain-general comparisons favored instruction first.
- **Verdict: partially supported.** A bounded prediction or attempted solution can prepare instruction;
  endless Socratic questioning without necessary prerequisite teaching is not justified by this result.

## P8 — Adapt to evidence and access needs, not fixed learning-style labels

Clinton-Lisell & Litzinger (10 July 2024), *Frontiers in Psychology*, peer-reviewed meta-analysis.
[Publisher/full text](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1428732/full).

- **Inspected:** abstract and discussion, including study-quality and crossover-interaction caveats.
- **Design:** 21 studies/101 effects/1,712 participants.
- **Finding:** a small average matching effect was reported, but crossover support was infrequent and study quality low.
  Authors judged the evidence insufficient for widespread matching-to-style adoption.
- **Verdict: supported.** Offer modality choices and accommodations; do not diagnose a permanent “visual learner” type.
  The defensible conclusion is not “all individual differences are irrelevant.”

## N1 — Synaptic and structural plasticity

Sung, Lee, Jung & Kaang (12 March 2026), *Journal of Neurochemistry* 170:e70404, peer-reviewed review.
[Full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12980114/); DOI 10.1111/jnc.70404.

- **Inspected:** abstract and introduction, especially adult plasticity and learning-related synaptic turnover.
- **Finding:** memory-related change involves synaptic strength as well as formation/elimination of connections.
- **Limit:** much mechanistic evidence comes from animal preparations and specific tasks; the review is not a trial
  of a teaching interface and does not yield a universal study duration or observable “plasticity score.”
- **Verdict: supported mechanistically.** Adult learning is biologically plausible; successful app interactions
  are not measurements of synaptic change or evidence that anyone can master any domain on demand.

## N2 — Sleep and systems consolidation

Lutz, Harkotte & Born (online 28 August 2025; January 2026 issue), *Physiological Reviews* 106:363–483.
[DOI](https://doi.org/10.1152/physrev.00054.2024);
[institution-hosted full text](https://push-zb.helmholtz-munich.de/deliver.php?id=43052).

- **Inspected:** abstract; sections 2.1–2.2 and 4.2, including conflicting findings on abstraction.
- **Finding:** human/animal evidence supports active memory processing during sleep; replay and coordinated
  activity across brain systems are leading mechanisms. Retention and generalization are not interchangeable.
- **Limit:** details vary by memory/task and remain debated. This does not validate sleep-timed app interventions.
- **Verdict: supported.** Spread practice across days and avoid incentives to sacrifice sleep;
  delayed assessment measures something an immediate fluent answer cannot. No brain-state inference from click logs.

## M1 — A score needs validity evidence for its intended use

AERA, APA & NCME (2014), *Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing*, authoritative standards.
[Official open-access edition](https://www.testingstandards.net/open-access-files.html).

- **Inspected:** chapter 1, printed pp.11–13; chapter 3, pp.54–58; Standards 3.0–3.5, pp.63–65.
- **Finding:** score interpretations need supporting evidence; irrelevant language, timing and response-format demands
  can distort inference. Technology does not exempt assessments from validity, precision or fairness requirements.
- **Verdict: supported.** Define the capability, test conditions and scoring rubric before declaring mastery.
  A text response cannot by itself establish a practical laboratory skill or authorship of unsupervised work.

## M2 — Higher-education assessment under generative AI

Crompton & Burke (16 October 2026), *International Journal of Educational Technology in Higher Education* 23:56.
[Publisher/full text](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s41239-026-00627-4).

- **Inspected:** abstract and study-scope/method description.
- **Design:** systematic review of 72 studies, 24 countries and 20 disciplines; studies January 2024–June 2026.
- **Finding:** process-oriented, authentic, and oral/dialogical approaches recur in assessment redesign.
- **Limit:** qualitative studies were most common. This describes practices; it does not establish a superior
  assessment method or show that oral questioning detects external help reliably.
- **Verdict: supported for the descriptive claim.** Combine products with explanation, revision and follow-up tasks;
  validate the resulting evidence rather than equating “authentic” with automatically valid.

## M3 — Accessibility requirements are not optional measurement details

W3C, [Web Content Accessibility Guidelines 2.2](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/), normative web standard.
Relevant criteria: keyboard access, timing adjustment, visible/unobscured focus and target size.
These are accessibility requirements, not evidence of an educational effect size.
Use them alongside M1: remove unrelated interaction barriers while preserving the skill being assessed.

## Claims the review does not establish

| Proposed claim | Verdict | Defensible replacement |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Every learner will master any topic through the platform | Unverified | Support defined learners toward defined competencies; measure failures and gaps |
| Neuroplasticity proves the interface works | Contradicted as an inference | Mechanisms motivate research; behavioral studies test the product |
| Retrieval should replace all explanation | Contradicted as a general rule | Match retrieval, elaboration, instruction and application to the objective |
| The same quiz pass implies durable mastery | Unverified | Independent performance, later retention and transfer are distinct evidence |
| A beautiful simulation teaches more than a diagram | Unverified | Compare representations on the intended learning outcome |
| A slow answer reveals low intelligence or low mastery | Unverified | Separate subject performance, access needs, task difficulty and response conditions |
| A fixed success percentage or review interval is scientifically optimal for every skill | Unverified | Calibrate and evaluate task-specific policies |

## Search/access limits

No private library account was used and no paywall or CAPTCHA was bypassed. Publisher failures and abstract-only
checks are disclosed. Selected English-language searches and source chaining do not establish exhaustive coverage.
This review prioritizes direct implications over cataloguing all mechanisms or every educational-technology paper.
Recent does not mean stronger: controlled learning outcomes and suitable comparators matter more than publication year.
